Method

  1. One demand model per store × item, fitted on 104 weeks: log(orders) = a + b·(our price ÷ competitor price) + promo lift + festival / IPL / exam / monsoon / Navratri flags + temperature + rainfall + trend.
  2. Partial pooling. Each store's price coefficient is blended with the average for the same item category and location type, weighted by how precise it is (empirical Bayes). Pairs with little price history borrow strength from similar ones. Uncertainty comes from a 4-week moving-block bootstrap, which respects week-to-week correlation.
  3. Model selection. Four candidates (naive average, per-item regression, pooled regression, gradient boosting) compared by rolling-origin cross-validation on accuracy, bias, accuracy in price-change and promo weeks, and stability of the price-sensitivity estimate.
  4. Simulation. Baseline weekly demand = the model's prediction for the most recent 13 weeks at today's price and no promotion. Any scenario rescales that baseline through the price response, then computes revenue and contribution per channel (dine-in vs delivery).
  5. Recommendation. Search every menu price (ending in 9) inside the guardrails and pick the one with the highest expected weekly contribution, provided it adds ≥1% and beats holding today's price in ≥80% of 500 draws from the price-sensitivity uncertainty. Otherwise: hold.
  6. Stress tests. Tornado (price sensitivity, food cost, competitor price, volume, delivery mix, commission), Monte Carlo, competitor-reaction scenarios, guardrail frontier, cost pass-through and discount break-even.

Synthetic dataset (provided as CSV files)

  • weekly_sales.csv — 17,472 rows: store × item × week with list price, discount, net price, competitor average price, promo type, dine-in and delivery orders, revenue, unit food cost, packaging, commission, contribution.
  • stores.csv — city, tier, location type, competitor density, seats, delivery share, income and footfall indices, commission rate.
  • products.csv — category, veg / non-veg, base price, food-cost %, packaging cost.
  • competitors.csv, calendar.csv (festivals, IPL, exams, monsoon), weather.csv (city × week temperature and rain), price_events.csv (menu revisions, price tests).
  • ground_truth_elasticities.csv — the hidden truth, only for validating the method (you never have this in real life).

Built-in patterns to discover

  • Airport and highway customers are captive (low sensitivity); college-area and dense residential/high-street stores are highly price-sensitive.
  • Office stores dip in Diwali and Christmas weeks; college stores dip in exams and summer vacation; the Lonavala highway store spikes in monsoon; malls peak in festive season.
  • Non-veg demand falls ~28% in North-India stores during Navratri; cold drinks rise with temperature; rain pushes orders to delivery.
  • Food costs inflate ~5.5% a year with a dairy-cost spike in mid-2025 on cheese items; competitors raise prices about once a year and run short promotions.

Limits of this prototype

  • Items are modelled independently — no cannibalisation (e.g. a cheaper burger pulling orders from combos) or basket effects (fries attached to burgers).
  • Competitor reaction is a manual assumption, not predicted. Long-run effects (brand perception, habit) are not captured by weekly data.
  • Recommendations outside the range of prices seen in history are extrapolations; the confidence label and uncertainty band flag this. Before a full roll-out, validate with a controlled store test (half of matched stores change price for 8 weeks).
  • Not yet encoded: menu price architecture (combo cheaper than parts, size ladders), city-level price consistency on delivery apps, separate delivery-app pricing.
